Overview

Real data is messy! If done properly, most of the time in a data anlysis project is spent understanding the data, cleaning it and preprocessing it to the format you require for downstream analysis. Messiness comes in many different ways so just be aware that you need to visualize, ask questions and practice! Below you have some common examples you might encounter as well as some inisght into what one-hot encoding means.

The data

messy_gp_patients.csv holds 30 patients from a handful of GP practices, with their age, height and smoking status.

Download messy_gp_patients.csv and save it in your data folder, or take every file at once from the zip on the introduction page.

gp <- gp |>

    smoking_status = case_when(

5. What happens when you one-hot encode?

Most algorithms need numbers, so a categorical variable has to become numeric somehow. One-hot encoding gives each category its own 0/1 column.

gp_encoded <- dummy_cols(gp,
                         select_columns = "GP_practice",
                         remove_selected_columns = TRUE) #why is this important? 

ncol(gp)
#> [1] 5
ncol(gp_encoded)
#> [1] 9
# View(gp_encoded)

Five columns became nine: the practice column is gone and five indicator columns have taken its place.

Now try the same thing on the patient identifier:

gp_ids_encoded <- dummy_cols(gp,
                             select_columns = "patient_id",
                             remove_selected_columns = TRUE)

c(original = ncol(gp),
  practice_encoded = ncol(gp_encoded),
  id_encoded = ncol(gp_ids_encoded))
#>         original practice_encoded       id_encoded 
#>                5                9               34
ImportantThink about it
  1. How many columns did each encoding add?
  2. What do the 0 (cold) and the 1 (hot) actually mean?
  3. Why was collapsing the rare practices worth doing before encoding?
  4. Should every categorical variable be one-hot encoded? What do you think of a gender column with just Male and Female class? Should we hot-encode it?
NoteWorked answer

Encoding the practice took 5 columns to 9 — one column out, five in. Encoding the identifier took 5 to 34: one column out, thirty in, one per patient.

A 1 means “this patient is at this practice” and a 0 means “is not”. For the identifier, each column is 1 for exactly one person and 0 for the other twenty-nine, so it can only ever identify that individual. On a real extract of 50,000 patients it would produce 50,000 columns, and not one of them would apply to patient 50,001 — the only patient you actually care about predicting.

Collapsing first is what kept the practice encoding at five columns rather than eight, with every column backed by at least three patients. A column with one patient in it cannot support an estimate of anything; it can only be memorised.

So no. Being categorical does not make a variable worth encoding. Decide what a variable means — identifier, group, measurement — before deciding what to do with it.

ImportantThink about it

Why is replacing an impossible age with missing better than replacing it with zero — or than leaving it at 250?

NoteWorked answer

Because 0 is a number a model will believe. It would be treated as a newborn, pulling the mean age down and the age–outcome relationship with it. Leaving 250 is worse again, for the same reason in the other direction.

Missing is the only one of the three that is true. We know the recorded value is wrong and we do not know what it should have been — it could have been 25, or 50, or a mistyped date of birth. Missing says exactly that, and every later step can then decide what to do about it. Note the contrast with the height in metres: 1.72 could only have meant 172, so that one is corrected rather than discarded. Correcting a value you can infer is cleaning; inventing one you cannot is something else.

Three practices have a single patient. It is difficult to find any pattern in a practice with just one patient, so combine anything with fewer than three patients into an Other category. You can do it in many different ways; pandas has no single function for it (R’s forcats has fct_lump_min()), so we do it in two steps: count the patients per practice, then replace the rare ones.

counts = gp_py["GP_practice"].value_counts()
rare = counts[counts < 3].index

gp_py["GP_practice"] = gp_py["GP_practice"].where(
    ~gp_py["GP_practice"].isin(rare), "Other")

print(gp_py["GP_practice"].value_counts())

ImportantThink about it
  1. How many practices were there to begin with, and how many are left?
  2. Where did the three single-patient practices go — and look carefully, because the answer is not “into a new category”.
  3. What information did you just lose?
NoteWorked answer

Eight practices become five: Bournbrook 9, Cherry Tree 7, Dovecote Lane 6, Selly Oak 3, and Other 5.

The extract already had an Other category with two patients, and Harborne, Edgbaston and Kings Heath were merged into it rather than into a second one. Two plus three is the five you see. That is worth checking every time you group rare levels, because an Other that already exists and an Other you create mean different things and will be silently combined.

What you lose is the identity of those three practices. If one of them had an unusual case mix — a practice serving a care home, say — that signal is now averaged into a category whose only shared property is being small. What you gain is five columns instead of eight when you encode, each supported by at least three patients.

ImportantThink about it
  1. Why standardise the labels before encoding or analysing them?
  2. Never and never differed by one capital letter. What would a model have done with them?
  3. The recipe above lower-cases and trims before matching. Why is that order necessary rather than tidy?
NoteWorked answer

Because a category is only a category if it is spelled one way. Left alone, Never and never are two different values: a frequency table reports 8 and 4 instead of 12, a bar chart shows two bars, and one-hot encoding produces two columns — each with a smaller count than the real category, so each looks rarer and weaker than it is. The model never learns they are the same thing; it cannot, because nothing in the data says so.

Lower-casing and trimming first turns Never, never and a stray never into one string before any matching happens. Match first and you would need an entry for every spelling, and you would miss the one you did not think of. Cleaning the form of a value and then matching on meaning is the order that scales.
