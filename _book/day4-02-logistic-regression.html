14  Exercise 4.2 — Probability, odds, and the fitted curve

14.1 Objectives

  • Convert between a probability, its odds and its log-odds, in both directions, without looking up the formula.
  • Fit a logistic regression in either language and read an odds ratio off the coefficient.
  • Produce the predicted probability for one named patient by hand from the coefficients, and confirm it with predict().

14.2 Why this, here

Chapter 13 fitted four classifiers and scored them all with accuracy, which is a property of a whole test set. No clinician has ever treated a test set. The object a clinician meets is a single patient with a single number attached, and for logistic regression that number is a probability — which is why this is the model the booklet uses to learn classification, long before the more accurate ones.

Getting from a linear model to a probability needs one idea, and the idea is arithmetic rather than statistics.

14.3 Probability, odds, log-odds

  • R
  • Python
p <- 0.8
odds <- p / (1 - p)
logit <- log(odds)
c(probability = p, odds = odds, log_odds = logit)
#> probability        odds    log_odds 
#>    0.800000    4.000000    1.386294
import numpy as np

p = 0.8
odds = p / (1 - p)
logit = np.log(odds)
{"probability": p, "odds": odds, "log_odds": round(float(logit), 4)}
#> {'probability': 0.8, 'odds': 4.000000000000001, 'log_odds': 1.3863}

Three names for the same belief, on three different scales.

  • A probability lives in \([0, 1]\).
  • The odds, \(p/(1-p)\), live in \([0, \infty)\). A probability of 0.8 is odds of 4, usually said as “four to one on”.
  • The log-odds or logit, \(\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\), live on the whole real line.

Only the third is a scale a linear model can safely be written on: a linear predictor is unbounded, and so are the log-odds. That single sentence is the whole reason logistic regression has the form it does.

\[\operatorname{logit}(p) = \log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\]

and running it backwards gives the logistic (sigmoid) function

\[p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x)}}.\]

NoteThis bit has no language

The two equations above are algebra, so there is no R version and no Python version of them. Everything below is both. Deriving the second from the first takes three lines and is the only derivation in today’s material; do it once, on paper, and you will never again be unsure which way round exp goes.

14.4 Fit it, in both languages, to the same rows

data/diabetes.csv again, all 768 patients, one predictor. We are not splitting here, and the reason is not laziness: this exercise reads the shape of a fitted curve and the meaning of a coefficient, neither of which is a claim about unseen patients. The moment we make such a claim — the next exercise — the split comes back.

  • R
  • Python
library(readr); library(dplyr); library(tidymodels)

diabetes <- read_csv("data/diabetes.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(diabetes = factor(diabetes, levels = c("neg", "pos")))

fit_glucose <- logistic_reg() |>
  set_engine("glm") |>
  fit(diabetes ~ glucose, data = diabetes)

tidy(fit_glucose)
#> # A tibble: 2 × 5
#>   term        estimate std.error statistic  p.value
#>   <chr>          <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>    <dbl>
#> 1 (Intercept)  -5.10     0.400       -12.8 3.09e-37
#> 2 glucose       0.0355   0.00302      11.8 5.89e-32
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

diabetes = pd.read_csv("data/diabetes.csv")
y = (diabetes["diabetes"] == "pos").astype(int)
X = diabetes[["glucose"]]

fit_glucose = LogisticRegression(C=np.inf, max_iter=1000).fit(X, y)
float(fit_glucose.intercept_[0]), float(fit_glucose.coef_[0][0])
#> (-5.0953569044194404, 0.03548515592234879)

Both give an intercept of −5.09536 and a glucose coefficient of 0.035485. They agree to every digit printed because they are now solving the same problem: C=np.inf switches off the L2 penalty that scikit-learn applies by default, and which R’s glm never applies at all (Chapter 13).

WarningC=np.inf, and what to do if your scikit-learn complains

Older versions of scikit-learn spell the same thing penalty=None, and versions from 1.8 deprecate that spelling in favour of C=np.inf. If you leave the argument out altogether you will get a slightly different, penalised fit and the two languages will stop agreeing — on this dataset in the fourth decimal place, on a small one in the first.

The fitted model is therefore

\[\operatorname{logit}\big(P(\text{diabetes} = \text{pos} \mid \text{glucose})\big) = -5.095 + 0.0355 \times \text{glucose}.\]

14.5 Task 1 — Say the coefficient out loud

A log-odds coefficient is not interpretable as it stands, and 0.0355 per mg/dL is not a sentence anyone would say. Exponentiating turns it into an odds ratio, and a 10 mg/dL step is a clinically sensible unit. Produce the odds ratio for a 10 mg/dL rise in glucose.

TipStuck?

The coefficient is on the log-odds scale and you want the odds scale. One function undoes a logarithm.

Write the sentence the number supports, in full: every 10 mg/dL of glucose multiplies the odds of diabetes by about 1.43. Then write what it does not support. It is not a risk ratio, it is not an effect of glucose on anything, and it is not a 43% increase in the probability — which the next task makes uncomfortably concrete.

14.6 Task 2 — One patient, by hand and by machine

A patient presents with a glucose of 120. Compute their log-odds, odds and probability from the two coefficients, using nothing but arithmetic. Then ask the fitted object for the same probability and check the two agree.

  • Python

        prob = prob_120, by_machine = by_machine), 4)
check_metric_range(prob_120, 0.25, 0.35, "P(pos | glucose = 120)")
# Replace every ______ below, then run this cell.

b0, b1 = fit_glucose.intercept_[0], fit_glucose.coef_[0][0]

log_odds_120 = b0 + b1 * ______
odds_120     = np.exp(log_odds_120)
prob_120     = 1 / (1 + np.exp(______))

print(np.round([log_odds_120, odds_120, prob_120, by_machine], 4))
check_metric_range(prob_120, 0.25, 0.35, "P(pos | glucose = 120)")

The answer is a probability of about 0.302: log-odds −0.837, odds 0.433. Now compare it with the sentence from Task 1. The odds ratio of 1.43 per 10 mg/dL is constant for every patient; the change in probability for the same 10 mg/dL is not. Going from 120 to 130 moves the probability by about 8 percentage points; going from 60 to 70 moves it by about 2. The model is linear in the log-odds and in nothing else, which is exactly what the sigmoid’s shape is telling you.

TipStuck?

In R the probability of the event is the column predict(type = "prob") named after the level you want. In Python predict_proba returns one column per class, in the order given by the estimator’s classes_ attribute — print it before you choose an index.

Two features of this plot deserve a sentence each. The curve never reaches 0 or 1: at the lowest real glucose in the file, 57 mg/dL, the model still gives 0.044, and it will not tell you a patient is safe. And there are five points at glucose = 0, which is not a glucose a living patient has; the file codes some missing values as zeros, which Chapter 5 takes apart.

14.8 Task 4 — Which column did you take?

Half of all logistic regression errors are taking the probability of the wrong class. There is a check for it, and the reason the check works is worth knowing: a logistic regression with an intercept, scored on the rows it was fitted to, produces fitted probabilities whose mean equals the observed prevalence exactly. Take the other column and you get one minus it.

  • R
  • Python
# Replace every ______ below, then run this cell.
source("checks/checks.R")
p_pos <- predict(fit_glucose, new_data = diabetes, type = "prob")$______
check_probabilities(p_pos, prevalence = mean(diabetes$diabetes == "pos"))
# Replace every ______ below, then run this cell.
import sys; sys.path.append("checks")
from checks import *
print("classes_:", fit_glucose.classes_)
p_pos = fit_glucose.predict_proba(X)[:, ______]
check_probabilities(p_pos, prevalence=float(y.mean()))

Run it deliberately wrong once — take .pred_neg, or column 0 — and read what the check says. A check you have never seen fail is a check you do not trust.

14.9 The result that should bother you

The original course notes for this material relevel the outcome so that pos comes first, on the reasonable grounds that pos is the event of interest. Do that, and the coefficient on glucose becomes negative, while the predicted probability of diabetes for every patient stays exactly the same.

library(forcats)
relevelled <- diabetes |> mutate(diabetes = fct_relevel(diabetes, "pos"))
fit_relevelled <- logistic_reg() |> set_engine("glm") |>
  fit(diabetes ~ glucose, data = relevelled)

round(c(original   = coef(fit_glucose$fit)[["glucose"]],
        relevelled = coef(fit_relevelled$fit)[["glucose"]]), 5)
#>   original relevelled 
#>    0.03549   -0.03549
p_orig <- predict(fit_glucose,    new_data = diabetes,   type = "prob")$.pred_pos
p_relv <- predict(fit_relevelled, new_data = relevelled, type = "prob")$.pred_pos
max(abs(p_orig - p_relv))
#> [1] 1.110223e-15
  1. glm() models the probability of the last level of the factor. With levels neg, pos it models pos, so the coefficient is +0.0355: more glucose, more diabetes. Relevel, and the last level is neg, so the same engine now models the probability of not having diabetes and reports −0.0355.
  2. parsnip knows this. It asks the engine for its own event, then returns both columns correctly labelled, which is why .pred_pos is identical to fifteen decimal places either way.
  3. So the coefficient’s sign and the column’s name follow two different conventions, and the published page that made this change kept a sentence underneath saying the model was \(\operatorname{logit}(P(\text{pos})) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\). With pos relevelled first, that sentence was false and the plot above it was right.

The transferable rule: never read a sign off a coefficient without checking which class the engine thinks it is modelling. The cheap way to check is the one you already have — predict for a patient with a high glucose and see which direction the probability went.

14.10 Reflection

  1. The odds ratio is constant and the probability difference is not. Which of the two would you put in a patient-facing leaflet, and what would you have to add to make it honest?
  2. This model has an accuracy you have not computed and a probability for every patient that you have. Which of those two would change if the study had recruited twice as many pos patients, and why does that matter for a model fitted on a case-control sample?

14.11 Stretch

NoteStretch 1 — Where is the curve steepest?

Find the glucose value at which the predicted probability is 0.5, from the two coefficients alone: it is \(-\beta_0/\beta_1\). Ours is about 144 mg/dL. This is the point at which the model is least sure, and — as Chapter 15 will show — it is also exactly what you are choosing when you choose a threshold of 0.5.

NoteStretch 2 — Add a predictor, and watch the coefficient move

Refit with diabetes ~ glucose + mass + age. The glucose coefficient changes a little (0.0355 to about 0.0328) and the intercept moves a long way (−5.10 to about −6.74). Explain why the second of those is arithmetic rather than news, and what the first one would have to look like before you called glucose confounded by age.

14.12 What you can and cannot now claim

Can: that for a patient drawn from this population, the model gives a probability of diabetes, and that you can produce and interpret that probability on all three scales.

Cannot: that any patient has diabetes. The model has not said so and cannot: a probability is not a decision, and nothing in the fitting procedure knows what it costs to be wrong in either direction. Turning 0.302 into a yes or a no is a separate act with separate consequences, and it is Chapter 15.
