38.1 Principles the later days should keep

These are what make the booklet a course rather than a pile of notebooks.

  1. Every exercise carries a control. A student who has only run a pipeline believes its output. One who has watched the same pipeline survive — or fail — a baseline, a shuffled label, or a no-shift extract believes the method. Day 1 has three controls: the predict-the-mean baseline, extract A, and the refitted-coefficient comparison.
  2. Nothing is introduced before the object it acts on. Cross-validation is only meaningful once a student has felt a single split wobble.
  3. Each exercise ends with what cannot be claimed. The limitation is not a disclaimer; it is the hook for the next day.
  4. Both languages, every time. Where they genuinely differ, say so and say why, rather than presenting one as a translation of the other.
  5. The provenance of every dataset is written down. Day 1’s data is simulated and its generating process is published, which is the only way a student can discover that their diagnosis was wrong. Days 2 to 6 use a synthetic diabetes dataset and two small clinical teaching files, documented in ?sec-data — including why the dataset the exercises were originally written against is no longer used.
  6. Every day fits on twenty printed pages. The site carries the discussion; the worksheet carries the tasks. If a day will not fit, the narrative moves into a ::: {.web-only} block rather than the tasks being cut.
