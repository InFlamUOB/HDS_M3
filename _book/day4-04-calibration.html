16  Exercise 4.4 — Is a predicted 0.7 worth 0.7?

ImportantA suggested addition, not part of the original module

This exercise was proposed and drafted by the assistant that rebuilt the booklet, not ported from an earlier version of the module. Exercise 4.3 names calibration as something it has not checked and cannot claim, and the booklet never returns to it. A probability used to make a decision is worth nothing unless it means what it says. Treat it as a draft to accept, rewrite or delete: nothing else depends on it, and removing it means deleting this file and its line in _quarto.yml.

16.1 Objectives

  • Build a calibration table by hand — bin the predicted probabilities, and put the mean prediction in each bin next to the rate actually observed in it.
  • Say why a model can be perfectly ranked and badly calibrated, and name a metric that notices the difference.
  • Decide whether a gap between predicted and observed is a fault in the model or the size of the test set.

16.2 Why this, here

Chapter 15 chose a threshold of 0.17 from a cost ratio of five to one, and the arithmetic behind that choice assumed something it never checked: that a predicted probability of 0.17 belongs to a group of patients of whom about 17% really do have diabetes. If the probabilities are on a different scale from the events, the threshold is still a number but it is no longer the number the cost ratio implies.

That assumption has a name. A model is calibrated when, among the patients it gives probability \(q\), the long-run proportion with the outcome is \(q\). Calibration is a property of the probabilities themselves, and it is not measured by anything Day 4 has computed so far: accuracy, sensitivity and specificity all throw the probability away at the threshold and look only at the classes that come out.

NoteOne number borrowed from Day 5

Task 3 needs the area under the ROC curve, which is Chapter 18 and has not been taught yet. You need only one fact about it here: AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive patient is given a higher score than a randomly chosen negative one. It is a statement about order and nothing else. That is the whole point of Task 3, and you can take the number on trust until Day 5 explains it.

The first check passes to six decimal places and it could not have done anything else: Chapter 14 showed that a logistic regression with an intercept makes the mean of its fitted probabilities equal the observed prevalence, exactly, on the rows it was fitted to. That is an algebraic identity, not evidence.

The second check reports [fix]. The mean predicted probability on the held-out patients is 0.386, the prevalence among them is 0.359, and the gap of 0.027 is larger than the check’s tolerance of 0.02. Nothing is broken: the training prevalence is 0.366 and the test prevalence is 0.359, so the model is carrying the training rate onto a test set that happens to be slightly healthier. This is the weakest possible calibration claim — calibration in the large, one number against one number — and the model already misses it by two and a half percentage points on 153 patients.

A model can pass that test and still be useless, because it averages. A model that gave every patient 0.359 would pass it perfectly.

16.4 Task 1 — The calibration curve, before a package draws one

Split the predicted probabilities into five bins of width 0.2. In each bin, count the patients, average the predictions, and compute the proportion who actually had diabetes. A calibrated model puts those last two columns on top of each other.

TipStuck?

The bins are five equal slices of the interval from 0 to 1. In R one function turns a numeric vector into a factor of intervals; in Python multiplying by the number of bins and truncating does the same job, with the top edge needing care because a probability of exactly 1 would otherwise fall into a sixth bin.

Both languages give the same five rows, because the split and the model are fixed:

Bin Patients Mean predicted Observed rate
0.0–0.2 40 0.146 0.125
0.2–0.4 57 0.297 0.298
0.4–0.6 23 0.474 0.522
0.6–0.8 17 0.703 0.647
0.8–1.0 16 0.842 0.625

The middle of the range is close. The top bin is not: the model promises 0.84 and delivers 0.63, which in a clinic is the difference between “almost certainly” and “probably”. The largest gap is 0.217.

16.5 Is that top bin a fault, or is it sixteen patients?

Before reading anything into a gap, ask what gap you would see from a model that was perfectly calibrated. We can answer that exactly, because we can generate outcomes from the model’s own probabilities and rebuild the table. This reuses the calibration_table() you wrote in Task 1, so run that cell first.

  • R
  • Python
set.seed(1)
max_gap <- function(y) {
  tab <- calibration_table(p_logit, factor(ifelse(y == 1, "pos", "neg"),
                                           levels = c("neg", "pos")))
  max(abs(tab$mean_predicted - tab$observed_rate), na.rm = TRUE)
}
simulated <- replicate(2000, max_gap(rbinom(length(p_logit), 1, p_logit)))

round(c(observed      = max_gap(as.integer(test$diabetes == "pos")),
        sim_median    = median(simulated),
        sim_90th      = unname(quantile(simulated, 0.90)),
        p_value       = mean(simulated >= max_gap(as.integer(test$diabetes == "pos")))), 3)
#>   observed sim_median   sim_90th    p_value 
#>      0.217      0.126      0.217      0.116
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

def max_gap(y):
    tab = calibration_table(p_logit, y)
    return float((tab["mean_predicted"] - tab["observed_rate"]).abs().max())

observed = max_gap(y_test.to_numpy())
simulated = np.array([max_gap(rng.binomial(1, p_logit)) for _ in range(2000)])

{"observed": round(observed, 3),
 "sim_median": round(float(np.median(simulated)), 3),
 "sim_90th": round(float(np.quantile(simulated, 0.90)), 3),
 "p_value": round(float((simulated >= observed).mean()), 3)}
#> {'observed': 0.217, 'sim_median': 0.126, 'sim_90th': 0.217, 'p_value': 0.117}

A perfectly calibrated model on 153 patients in five bins produces a largest gap of 0.126 on a typical run, and exceeds the 0.217 we measured in about 12% of runs. The curve is not evidence that this model is miscalibrated. It is mostly evidence that sixteen patients is not many, and it is the reason calibration plots in papers should carry the bin counts — which is why calibration_table() returns n first.

TipStuck?

The forest’s probability column has the same name as the logistic regression’s, because parsnip names it after the factor level; in Python predict_proba again returns one column per class in classes_ order. The second blank is the column of calibration_table() that holds what actually happened.

The warning does not come true here, and that is worth more than if it had. The forest’s probabilities reach both ends of the interval — 0.008 to 0.906 in R, 0.004 to 0.924 in Python — rather than huddling near the prevalence, its mean is 0.362 against a prevalence of 0.359 — it passes the check the logistic regression failed — and its largest bin gap is 0.19 in R and 0.30 in Python. Neither model is distinguishable from calibrated at this sample size, and the two languages disagree about which looks worse, which tells you how much signal there is in the comparison.

Pile-up towards the middle is real, but it is a property of small or heavily regularised ensembles. Five hundred unrestricted trees on 615 patients are confident enough to reach both ends.

16.7 Task 3 — Same order, different numbers

Now break the probabilities on purpose, in a way no ranking metric can see. Shrink every prediction a third of the way towards 0.5:

\[q = 0.5 + \frac{p - 0.5}{3}.\]

This is strictly increasing in \(p\), so the patients come out in exactly the same order. Compute the AUC before and after, and the calibration table after.

  • Python

The Brier score in that cell is the mean squared difference between the probability and the outcome coded 0 or 1. Unlike AUC it is a proper scoring rule: it is minimised by telling the truth, so it falls when the ranking improves and also when the numbers themselves get closer to the events.

16.8 The result that should bother you

The squashed model and the original model are the same model. One is a one-line transformation of the other, and every patient keeps their position in the queue.

  1. The AUC is 0.742022 before and 0.742022 after — identical to six decimal places, in both languages, because AUC counts pairs in the right order and the order did not change. A report that quotes only AUC cannot tell these two models apart, and most published model reports quote only AUC.
  2. The Brier score rises from 0.199 to 0.218, and the probabilities now span 0.359 to 0.625. Every patient is told something between “a bit more likely than not to be fine” and “not far off even odds”, which is a model with nothing to say.
  3. Apply Chapter 15’ cost-minimising threshold of 0.17 to each. The original probabilities send 123 of the 153 patients for investigation at a cost of 74. The squashed probabilities send all 153, at a cost of 98 — exactly the cost of ignoring the model and investigating everybody. The entire value Day 4.3 extracted from this model has been destroyed by a transformation that AUC says never happened.
  • R
  • Python
cost_of <- function(p, threshold, cost_fn = 5, cost_fp = 1) {
  called_pos <- p >= threshold
  truly_pos  <- test$diabetes == "pos"
  cost_fn * sum(!called_pos & truly_pos) + cost_fp * sum(called_pos & !truly_pos)
}
p_squashed <- 0.5 + (p_logit - 0.5) / 3

for (threshold in c(0.17, 0.5)) {
  print(rbind(
    raw      = c(threshold = threshold, n_investigated = sum(p_logit    >= threshold),
                 cost = cost_of(p_logit,    threshold)),
    squashed = c(threshold = threshold, n_investigated = sum(p_squashed >= threshold),
                 cost = cost_of(p_squashed, threshold))))
}
#>          threshold n_investigated cost
#> raw           0.17            123   74
#> squashed      0.17            153   98
#>          threshold n_investigated cost
#> raw            0.5             40  165
#> squashed       0.5             40  165
truly_pos = y_test.to_numpy() == 1
p_squashed = 0.5 + (p_logit - 0.5) / 3

def cost_of(p, threshold, cost_fn=5, cost_fp=1):
    called_pos = p >= threshold
    return int(cost_fn * (~called_pos & truly_pos).sum()
               + cost_fp * (called_pos & ~truly_pos).sum())

pd.DataFrame([
    dict(model=name, threshold=t, n_investigated=int((p >= t).sum()), cost=cost_of(p, t))
    for t in (0.17, 0.5)
    for name, p in [("raw", p_logit), ("squashed", p_squashed)]
])
#>       model  threshold  n_investigated  cost
#> 0       raw       0.17             123    74
#> 1  squashed       0.17             153    98
#> 2       raw       0.50              40   165
#> 3  squashed       0.50              40   165

The sting is in the two rows for 0.5. At that threshold the two models are indistinguishable — both call 40 patients positive and both cost 165 — because 0.5 is the one point this transformation leaves alone. A team that had never moved off the default would have found nothing wrong, and would have been right that nothing was wrong for them. Calibration starts to matter the moment the threshold stops being arbitrary.

16.9 Reflection

  1. AUC did not move and the Brier score did. Write the one-sentence rule you would give a colleague for when AUC alone is an adequate summary of a clinical prediction model, and when it is not.
  2. The noise simulation said we cannot tell this model from a calibrated one on 153 patients. How many patients would you want before you were willing to publish a calibration curve with five bins, and what would you report alongside it if you could not get them?

16.10 Stretch

NoteStretch 1 — Fix the imbalance, break the probabilities

The booklet has quoted a prevalence near 36% since Chapter 4 and never resampled it. Try it: keep all 225 pos training patients, draw an equal number of neg at random, refit diabetes ~ glucose on the balanced 450, and predict the same 153 test patients.

Nothing happens to the ranking. With one predictor the fitted probability is a monotone function of glucose whichever rows you fitted on, so the test AUC comes back as 0.742022 again, to the last digit. Everything happens to the scale: the mean predicted probability rises from 0.386 to about 0.49, against a prevalence of 0.359, and the Brier score rises from 0.199 to about 0.212. Chapter 15’ threshold of 0.17 now costs 94 or 95 instead of 74, because on the balanced model 0.17 is a glucose of about 83 rather than about 100 and almost every patient clears it.

The model is not worse. It is answering a different question — the risk in a population where diabetes is half as common as it is here is not the risk in this one — and the threshold was measured on the old scale. Re-minimising the cost on the new probabilities finds 0.26 or 0.27, and the cost there is 74 again: the same decisions, relabelled. Ask what you would have had to know to spot this if someone handed you only the resampled model and its AUC.

NoteStretch 2 — Put it back

Fit a logistic regression of the outcome on the log-odds of the squashed predictions, using the test set’s own outcomes, and look at the slope. This is Platt scaling, and on a monotone distortion it undoes the damage almost exactly. Then say why doing it on the test set, as you just did, is not allowed, and what you would have had to hold back at the start to do it properly.

16.11 What you can and cannot now claim

Can: that you can build and read a calibration table for a set of predicted probabilities, that you can say whether its wobbles are larger than the sampling noise of the test set, and that you know one metric which notices a distortion that AUC cannot.

Cannot: that this model is calibrated. The honest finding is weaker and more useful: on 153 held-out patients it is not detectably miscalibrated, which is a statement about the test set at least as much as about the model. Nor can you claim that calibration survives deployment — Chapter 3’s covariate shift moves the case mix, and a model calibrated in development is routinely not calibrated in the clinic.
