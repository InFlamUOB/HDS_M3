13  Day 3 — Overview

Day 1 fitted a model and Day 2 cleaned the data it was fitted to. Both treated the fitting itself as a single function call: fit() in, coefficients out. Today we open that call. By the end of the day you will have found the same two numbers four times — by lm(), by searching a grid of candidates, by walking downhill on the loss surface, and by algebra — and you will be able to say which of those a computer actually does and why.

The thread running through the day is one deliberately unpromising clinical question:

Does a patient’s age tell you anything about their blood glucose?

The answer, on these data, is: almost nothing. That is the point. An exercise where the model works teaches you the mechanics; an exercise where the mechanics are perfect and the model is still useless teaches you the difference between the two.

NoteEverything today is in-sample

We fit on all 80 rows and score on the same 80 rows, with no split anywhere. After Chapter 3 that should feel wrong, and it is — none of today’s numbers is evidence that anything generalises. Today’s question is what fitting does, not how well the result predicts. Held-out evaluation returns on Day 5.

13.1 Objectives

By the end of the day you should be able to, without looking anything up:

  • Distinguish the learning algorithm, the fitted parameters and the dataset, and say which of the three a prediction depends on.
  • Compute SSR, MSE, RMSE and MAE from a set of residuals, and say what squaring does to a badly mispredicted patient that taking the absolute value does not.
  • Show that the best fit line depends on the loss you chose, by fitting the same data under two losses and getting two different lines.
  • Write the gradient descent update rule from memory, and diagnose from a loss curve whether the learning rate is too large, too small, or about right.
  • Derive the ordinary least squares estimators for one predictor and verify them against lm().

13.2 Shape of the day

Exercise Time Mode
3.1 Supervised learning 45 min individual
3.2 Loss functions 50 min individual
— break 15 min
3.3 Gradient descent 60 min individual
3.4 OLS derivation 40 min individual, then pairs
— Consolidation 20 min plenary

Do them in order; the argument is cumulative. 3.2 defines what 3.1 left undefined, 3.3 solves what 3.2 could only search, and 3.4 shows that for this one model none of 3.3 was needed.

13.3 What you need before you start

  • R and Python working locally, and this project open in your editor (Appendix A).
  • data/diabetes.csv, which is the module’s case study and a synthetic replacement for a withdrawn dataset (Section B.5). Today we use the first 80 rows with a non-zero glucose.
  • From Module 2: what a derivative is. You will differentiate \(x^2\), a cubic and a function of two variables. Nothing harder appears, and Appendix D defines every term the day introduces.
