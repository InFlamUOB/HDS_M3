4  Exercise 1.3 — When the patients change

60 minutes · pairs, then plenary

4.1 Objectives

  • Evaluate a fixed model on data it was not trained on, without refitting it.
  • Distinguish a change in who the patients are from a change in how the outcome arises, using evidence from the data rather than the story.
  • Say which of the two a given model can survive, and why.
  • Name the one kind of shift you can detect without ever seeing an outcome.

4.2 The situation

Your model from Chapter 3 went into use. Twelve months later three monthly extracts arrive from the data warehouse: extract_A.csv, extract_B.csv, extract_C.csv, 400 admissions each, same three columns.

Nobody tells you what happened in between. Exactly one of these is true of each extract:

no shift same patients, same relationship. The model should still work.
covariate shift the patients changed — \(P(X)\) is different — but the relationship between predictors and outcome, \(P(Y \mid X)\), is unchanged.
covariate and concept shift the patients changed and the relationship changed.

That distinction is the exercise, so be clear before you start. Covariate shift means you are being asked about different people. Concept shift means the rules moved: the same patient would now have a different length of stay. A model is a frozen claim about \(P(Y \mid X)\). When only \(P(X)\) moves the claim may still be true. When \(P(Y \mid X)\) moves it is false, and no amount of data about the new patients will tell you so unless you also have their outcomes.

4.3 The model you are testing

Each page of this booklet is a fresh session, so the cell below rebuilds the model exactly as you fitted it in Chapter 3 — same data, same seed. Run it, then leave it alone. Do not refit it on the new extracts. The whole point is that the model is frozen and the world moved.

library(readr); library(dplyr); library(ggplot2)
library(parsnip); library(rsample); library(yardstick)

los <- read_csv("data/los_data.csv")
set.seed(42)
split   <- initial_split(los, prop = 0.8)
los_fit <- linear_reg() |> set_engine("lm") |>
  fit(LOS ~ Age + Severity, data = training(split))

test_mae <- mae(bind_cols(predict(los_fit, testing(split)), testing(split)),
                truth = LOS, estimate = .pred)$.estimate
round(test_mae, 2)   # your reference number from Exercise 1.2
#> [1] 3.42

WarningThe error that will cost you ten minutes

predict() on a parsnip fit wants new_data =, with an underscore. Passing newdata = silently predicts on the training data and returns the wrong number of rows.

Write down which extracts have a different case-mix from the original. This question has a clean answer, and it is not the same as the answer to Task 1.

NoteMake it a number, not an impression

Density plots are a good way to see a shift and a bad way to report one. Add one quantitative statement per extract — the difference in means expressed in standard deviations of the original, or the proportion of new patients outside the 1st–99th percentile range of the training ages. “The curves look different” is not a finding.

4.7 Task 3 — Did the rules change?

This is the task that settles the exercise. For each extract, refit a linear model on that extract alone and compare its coefficients with the original. You are no longer asking how well the old model predicts; you are asking whether the relationship it encodes is still the relationship in the data.

coefs <- function(d) coef(lm(LOS ~ Age + Severity, data = d))
rbind(original = coefs(los), A = ______, B = ______, C = ______) |> round(2)
TipStuck on how to judge “different”?

Compare each coefficient against the spread of the original estimate. A change from 0.93 to 0.95 is noise; a change from 0.93 to 0.13 is not. To be precise, put a confidence interval round the original with confint(lm(LOS ~ Age + Severity, los)) and ask whether the extract’s estimate falls inside it.

4.9 The result that should bother you

One extract contains patients unlike anything in the training data — markedly younger, markedly sicker — and the frozen model predicts their length of stay about as accurately as it predicted the original test set.

Five minutes on why, because the reasoning transfers.

  1. The model is a straight line in age and severity, and the true relationship in that extract is the same straight line. Asking it about a 25-year-old is asking it to extrapolate, and a correctly specified linear model extrapolates correctly.
  2. So covariate shift is not inherently harmful. It is harmful when the model is wrong in exactly the region the new patients occupy — which you cannot check, because you had no patients there.
  3. Which is why “the input distribution has shifted” is a reason to look, not a verdict. Stretch 1 shows a model for which the same shift is damaging.
ImportantThe asymmetry to remember

You can detect covariate shift with no outcomes at all — age and severity arrive with the referral. You cannot detect concept shift without outcomes, because it is a statement about the relationship between inputs and outcome. In a deployed clinical model the outcomes arrive weeks or months late, so the dangerous shift is the one you learn about last (Finlayson et al. 2021).

4.10 Reflection

Answer in writing; this is the assessable part.

  1. Which extract would be most dangerous in clinical use, and why? “The one with the worst MAE” is only right if you can say why worst-MAE is the right criterion here.
  2. Your model is in use. You see Age and Severity for every new referral, and true length of stay only after discharge. Which of the three situations could you detect this week, and which would you learn about in three months?
  3. A colleague proposes refitting monthly on the most recent extract. Name one problem this solves and one it creates.

4.11 Stretch

NoteStretch 1 — The same shift, a different model

Refit Chapter 3’s decision tree on the original training data and run it on all three extracts. Linear regression lost nothing on the covariate-shifted extract; the tree roughly doubles its error. Explain why, in terms of what a tree does outside the range of its training data — then say which model you would rather deploy, and under what monitoring.

(Ours: tree test MAE ≈ 5.9, A ≈ 5.9, B ≈ 9.9, C ≈ 29.9.)

NoteStretch 2 — Design the monitoring

Specify what the hospital should monitor monthly to catch this automatically. Five lines: what is measured, on what data, compared with what reference, what threshold triggers a look, and who looks. Specific enough for a data engineer to build. Then mark your own specification against the one thing it cannot catch.

NoteStretch 3 — Read one of these

Futoma and colleagues argue that generalisability as usually claimed in health ML is a myth, and that models should be validated where they will be used (Futoma et al. 2020). Yang and colleagues measured it across hospital sites for COVID-19 screening (Yang et al. 2022). Read one; bring a sentence connecting it to extract C.

4.12 What you can and cannot now claim

Can: that this model’s accuracy depends on an assumption you can state precisely — that \(P(Y \mid X)\) is the same in deployment as in training — and that you can test it when outcomes are available and partly monitor it when they are not.

Cannot: that a model surviving all three extracts is safe to deploy. These extracts contain the shifts we built into them. Real services shift in ways nobody simulated, including in response to the model itself.

Finlayson, Samuel G., Adarsh Subbaswamy, Karandeep Singh, et al. 2021. “The Clinician and Dataset Shift in Artificial Intelligence.” New England Journal of Medicine 385 (3): 283–86. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMc2104626.
Futoma, Joseph, Morgan Simons, Trishan Panch, Finale Doshi-Velez, and Leo Anthony Celi. 2020. “The Myth of Generalisability in Clinical Research and Machine Learning in Health Care.” The Lancet Digital Health 2 (9): e489–92. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2589-7500(20)30186-2.
