15  Exercise 4.3 — Choosing a threshold

15.1 Objectives

  • Turn predicted probabilities into predicted classes at a stated threshold, and build the confusion table that results.
  • Say what moving the threshold from 0.5 to 0.3 buys and what it costs, in patients rather than in percentages.
  • Choose a threshold from a stated cost ratio, and defend the choice against the threshold that maximises accuracy.

15.2 Why this, here

Chapter 14 ended with a probability of 0.302 for a patient with a glucose of 120. No clinic can act on 0.302. Somebody has to decide whether that patient is investigated further, and that decision needs a cut-off.

The cut-off is not part of the model. Nothing in the fitting procedure chose 0.5, no diagnostic accuracy study supports it, and the software offers it as a default only because it has to offer something. Choosing it properly requires one piece of information the data does not contain: what it costs to be wrong in each of the two directions. In diabetes screening those costs are not remotely equal — a missed diagnosis is years of untreated hyperglycaemia, a false alarm is a fasting glucose test and a worried afternoon — and the whole of this exercise follows from that asymmetry.

15.3 Fit on the training rows, predict on the held-out ones

Same split as Chapter 13: every fifth row held back, so that the two languages are working on the same 153 patients. This time we are making a claim about unseen patients, so the split is doing real work.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

diabetes = pd.read_csv("data/diabetes.csv")
is_test = (diabetes.index + 1) % 5 == 0
train, test = diabetes[~is_test], diabetes[is_test]

y_train = (train["diabetes"] == "pos").astype(int)
y_test  = (test["diabetes"] == "pos").astype(int)

fit = LogisticRegression(C=np.inf, max_iter=1000).fit(train[["glucose"]], y_train)
p_pos = fit.predict_proba(test[["glucose"]])[:, 1]

print(round(fit.intercept_[0], 6), round(fit.coef_[0][0], 6))
#> -5.308065 0.037362
print(len(test), int(y_test.sum()), int((y_test == 0).sum()))
#> 153 55 98

Both languages fit the same model to the same 615 patients and report an intercept of −5.308065 and a glucose coefficient of 0.037362. The test set is 153 patients, 55 of them pos. Every number below is therefore directly comparable between the two tabs, which was the point of fixing the split.

The predicted probabilities run from 0.078 to 0.874 and average 0.386.

15.4 A threshold is a rule, and a rule has consequences

library(ggplot2)
ggplot(preds, aes(x = glucose, y = .pred_pos, colour = diabetes)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.7, size = 1.6) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = c(0.3, 0.5, 0.7), linetype = "dashed") +
  labs(x = "Glucose (mg/dL)", y = "Predicted P(pos)", colour = NULL,
       caption = "Dashed lines: thresholds 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7")

Everything above a line is called positive, everything below it negative. With one predictor the picture is unusually honest: a probability threshold is a glucose cut-off, and these three are 119, 142 and 165 mg/dL. Any colour above a line that is not the positive class is a false alarm; any positive-class point below it is a missed diagnosis.

15.5 Task 1 — Three thresholds, three confusion tables

Count the four cells of the confusion table at 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7, and turn them into sensitivity, specificity and positive predictive value. Do it by counting first: the packaged functions come in the next section, and they are much easier to misuse than to use.

bind_rows(lapply(c(0.3, 0.5, 0.7), counts)) |>

         specificity = TN / (TN + FP),
         ppv         = TP / (TP + FP),

Read the FN column before anything else. At the default threshold the model misses 30 of the 55 patients who have diabetes. At 0.7 it misses 38 — more than two in three. Moving from 0.5 down to 0.3 finds 17 more of them and raises the false alarms from 15 to 38.

15.6 The packaged version, and the trap in it

Both languages ship a confusion-matrix function, and both have a convention about which class counts as the event. Getting it wrong silently swaps your sensitivity and your specificity.

  • R
  • Python
preds_0.5 <- preds |>
  mutate(predicted = factor(if_else(.pred_pos >= 0.5, "pos", "neg"),
                            levels = c("neg", "pos")))

conf_mat(preds_0.5, truth = diabetes, estimate = predicted)
#>           Truth
#> Prediction neg pos
#>        neg  83  30
#>        pos  15  25
c(default      = sens(preds_0.5, truth = diabetes, estimate = predicted)$.estimate,
  event_second = sens(preds_0.5, truth = diabetes, estimate = predicted,
                      event_level = "second")$.estimate)
#>      default event_second 
#>    0.8469388    0.4545455
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, recall_score

print(confusion_matrix(y_test, (p_pos >= 0.5).astype(int), labels=[0, 1]))
#> [[83 15]
#>  [30 25]]
{"pos_label=1": round(recall_score(y_test, (p_pos >= 0.5).astype(int)), 3),
 "pos_label=0": round(recall_score(y_test, (p_pos >= 0.5).astype(int), pos_label=0), 3)}
#> {'pos_label=1': 0.455, 'pos_label=0': 0.847}

yardstick treats the first factor level as the event, and our levels are neg, pos, so its default sensitivity of 0.847 is the specificity of the thing you care about. event_level = "second" recovers 0.455. scikit-learn makes the same decision with pos_label, which defaults to 1 and therefore happens to be right here — but only because we coded the outcome as 0/1 in that order.

The original course notes for this material converted the outcome to a 0/1 factor before calling conf_mat(), which made "0" the first level and the event. The table they printed was a correct table of the wrong event. It is a one-word fault, it does not raise an error, and the numbers it produces are plausible — which is the whole problem.

15.7 Task 2 — Put a price on the two errors

A missed diagnosis is not a false alarm. For this exercise, take a missed case to cost five times what a false alarm costs — the ratio is a decision for a service, not a statistician, and Task 3 asks you to argue with it.

Compute the total cost over a grid of thresholds, find the cheapest one, and compare it with two strategies that use no model at all: investigate everybody, and investigate nobody.

15.8 The result that should bother you

The default threshold is not merely suboptimal here. It is beaten by a policy with no model in it.

  1. At 0.5 the model’s accuracy is 0.706, against 0.641 for always saying neg. Six and a half percentage points for three decades of diabetes research, and a student reporting only accuracy would call that a working classifier.
  2. At 0.5 the same model misses 30 of 55 cases. Those two sentences describe the same 153 patients. Accuracy is high because the model is good at the common class, and the common class is the one nobody is screening for.
  3. Price the errors at 5:1 and the arithmetic turns hostile. The default threshold costs 165. Investigating every one of the 153 patients costs 98, because it incurs 98 false alarms and no missed cases. The model as deployed at its default is two-thirds worse than a policy that ignores it.

The model is not useless: at its cost-minimising threshold of 0.17 it costs 74, about a quarter less than investigating everybody. But that is the honest size of the win, it arrives only once somebody states the cost ratio, and it is invisible at 0.5.

NoteWhere 0.17 comes from

For a known cost ratio the optimal threshold is \(\text{cost}_{FP} / (\text{cost}_{FP} + \text{cost}_{FN})\) — here \(1/(1+5) = 0.167\) — whenever the predicted probabilities are well calibrated. Our grid finds 0.17 empirically on 153 patients, which is agreement to the nearest grid point and partly luck. The theoretical result is the thing to remember; the grid is what you run when you do not trust the calibration, which is most of the time.

15.9 Task 3 — Set the operating point, and defend it

Find the highest threshold at which sensitivity is at least 0.80, then report what it costs. Write two sentences for a diabetes service: one stating the rule in glucose rather than in probability, one stating the price.

The answer is 0.28, which catches 44 of the 55 cases and raises 43 false alarms. Its positive predictive value is 0.506: half the people this rule sends for investigation do not have diabetes, and its accuracy, 0.647, is almost exactly the do-nothing baseline. A service can accept that — screening programmes routinely do — but only if someone has said so out loud. Write the two sentences before you look at the next section.

15.10 Reflection

  1. Sensitivity rose from 0.455 to 0.800 and accuracy fell from 0.706 to 0.647. Explain to a sceptical colleague why that is an improvement, without using the word “cost”.
  2. Everything here was computed on 153 patients with the prevalence this file happens to have. Name one thing that changes about your chosen threshold if the model is deployed in a population where diabetes is half as common, and say which of the four numbers in the confusion table move first.

15.11 Stretch

NoteStretch 1 — All the thresholds at once

Plot sensitivity against 1 − specificity across the whole grid. You have just drawn a ROC curve by hand, and the area under it is the one classification summary that does not depend on the threshold — which is a virtue and a vice. Chapter 18 is the full treatment; arriving there having built the curve from counts is worth an hour of slides.

NoteStretch 2 — Choose the threshold honestly

Split the 615 training rows again into fit and validation parts, choose the threshold on the validation part, and only then report its cost on the test set. Does the cost go up? It usually does, and the gap is the price of having chosen the threshold on the data you then reported.

NoteStretch 3 — Does a better model move the threshold?

Refit with diabetes ~ glucose + mass + age and repeat Task 2. The cost at the best threshold falls; ask whether the best threshold itself moved, and whether you would have predicted the direction.

15.12 What you can and cannot now claim

Can: that at a stated threshold, on 153 held-out patients, this model finds a stated fraction of the diabetes in them at a stated false-alarm rate — and that you can produce the threshold a stated cost ratio implies.

Cannot: that any of these thresholds is right for a real service. The cost ratio was invented for the exercise, the prevalence is this file’s, the probabilities have not been checked for calibration, and the threshold was chosen on the same rows it was scored on. Each of those is a day of its own, and Chapter 18 is the next one.
