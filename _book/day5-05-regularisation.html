ImportantA suggested addition, not part of the original module

This exercise was proposed and drafted by the assistant that rebuilt the booklet, not ported from an earlier version of the module. The word regularisation appears nowhere else in the booklet, although the site this material came from was called RegularizationAndTrees. Day 5 shows a forest beating a tree by averaging variance away; penalties are the other answer to the same problem. Treat it as a draft to accept, rewrite or delete: nothing else depends on it, and removing it means deleting this file and its line in _quarto.yml.

Every cell on this page is a real R or Python cell, run against data/diabetes.csv with the split used throughout Day 5, plus four columns of random numbers added on purpose.

30.1 Objectives

  • Fit a lasso and a ridge logistic regression with the penalty chosen by cross-validation, in both languages, and read off which coefficients the penalty set to exactly zero.
  • Say what each of the two penalties does to a predictor that carries no signal, and defend a choice between them to a reader.
  • Show that the penalty which minimises cross-validated AUC is not the penalty that recovers the right variables, and quote the AUC the difference costs.

30.2 Why this, here

Chapter 29 answered the instability of a single tree by averaging: grow five hundred trees on resampled rows and let the variance cancel. There is a second answer to the same problem, and it does not need five hundred of anything. Leave one model, and make it harder for the fitting procedure to give any coefficient a large value. That is regularisation, and Chapter 20 already met it without arguing for it — scikit-learn applies a penalty by default, R’s glmnet engine applies one when asked, and the chapter closed by saying that Day 5 would make the case. This is that case, and the case against.

It sits here, after Chapter 11 and Chapter 28, because the strength of the penalty is a hyperparameter like tree_depth: there is no way to read it off the data you fitted on, and the only honest way to choose it is the resampling machinery you already have.

30.3 Worked example — twelve predictors, four of which are nothing

We take Day 5’s eight predictors and add four columns of random numbers, drawn independently of everything else and of each other. Chapter 33 uses one such control; four is better, because then the useless columns outnumber the predictors that carry any real signal in this data — which, as Day 6 will show, is roughly glucose and age.

Every predictor is standardised first. A penalty acts on the size of a coefficient, and the size of a coefficient depends on the units of its predictor, so penalising unstandardised predictors penalises whoever chose the units. The standardisation happens inside the recipe or the pipeline, which is what makes it honest under resampling (Chapter 10).

def coefs(fitted):
    """The coefficients of a fitted pipeline, named, largest first."""
    s = pd.Series(fitted[-1].coef_[0], index=X.columns)
    return s.reindex(s.abs().sort_values(ascending=False).index)

WarningTwo spellings of “no penalty”, and one relevel that differs from the rest of Day 5

LogisticRegression() always penalises unless you stop it: the default C=1.0 is a ridge penalty, and C=np.inf is the unpenalised fit that matches R’s glm (Chapter 20). If you are on scikit-learn 1.7 or earlier, write penalty=None, penalty="l1" and penalty="l2" where this page writes C=np.inf, l1_ratio=1 and l1_ratio=0; the older spellings still run on 1.8 and 1.9 but print a deprecation warning.

The factor levels here are neg, pos — the order Chapter 33 uses, not the pos, neg of Chapter 27. This page reads coefficients, and with neg first the coefficients are for the probability of pos, so a positive coefficient means higher risk. The price is event_level = "second" in every yardstick call below.

Nothing is wrong with either fit. This is what maximum likelihood does when it is given twelve predictors and 537 patients: it spends a little of each coefficient on whatever the random columns happen to line up with.

l1_ratio is the share of the penalty that is L1, and the lasso is all of it. The scoring string is the one GridSearchCV understood in Chapter 29.

Notepenalty and C run in opposite directions

R’s penalty is the penalty itself: larger means more shrinkage. Python’s C is its reciprocal: larger means less. The two are related by C = 1 / (n * penalty), with n = 537 training rows here, because glmnet divides the likelihood by the sample size and scikit-learn does not. R’s chosen penalty of 0.0085 is therefore a C of 0.22, and Python’s chosen C of 0.386 is a penalty of 0.0048. That is as close as two different splits of the same 768 patients were ever going to agree; the grids look unrelated and are not.

30.8 The result that should bother you

The lasso did set coefficients to exactly zero, which is what it is for. Look at which ones. In R it zeroed insulin and triceps — two measurements a diabetes clinic takes — and kept all four random columns. In Python it zeroed insulin, triceps and one of the four controls, and kept the other three.

Three steps.

  1. The penalty was chosen to minimise cross-validated AUC and nothing else. At the chosen penalty — 0.0085 in R and C = 0.386 in Python, which is a penalty of 0.0048 in R’s units — the resampled AUC is 0.777 with a standard error of 0.023, and 0.803 with a standard error of 0.015. A control column that happens to line up with the outcome in the training rows earns a small coefficient, and taking it away costs a little resampled AUC, so the tuning keeps it. Prediction was the objective. Selection was a side effect, and nobody asked the cross-validation to do it well.
  2. The penalty that does clear the controls sits further along the path: 0.0418 in R and C = 0.0788 in Python. It costs 0.007 and 0.006 of resampled AUC respectively, both comfortably inside one standard error, so the sparse model predicts just as well. But read the other column of the path table. Clearing the four worthless columns takes three real ones with it: the survivors fall from 7 to 4 in R and from 8 to 5 in Python.
  3. And the list that comes out is not stable. At that same penalty, over twenty bootstrap resamples of the same 537 patients, R kept age alone 6 times, pregnant alone 7 times, both 5 times and neither twice; Python kept pregnant alone 12 times, age alone once, both 5 times and neither twice. The two are correlated at about 0.74, the penalty will pay for one of them, and which one it buys depends on which patients were resampled.

A lasso hands you a variable list with no uncertainty attached to it. The list has at least as much sampling variability as any other estimate you have made this week, and it hides it better, because a coefficient of exactly zero looks like a decision rather than an estimate.

30.9 What the penalty assumes

A penalty is not a neutral correction for small samples. It is a statement about which coefficient values are plausible before the data arrives: an L2 penalty is exactly a normal prior on the coefficients centred at zero, and an L1 penalty is a Laplace prior, which is peaked at zero and has no derivative there — that kink is what produces exact zeros. Both say that a coefficient of 0.1 is more believable than one of 2, for every predictor equally.

Sometimes that is right. In a model with twelve standardised predictors and 537 patients, large coefficients usually are noise, and the test AUCs above say so. But it is a claim about the biology, and it is being made by the fitting procedure rather than by you. A penalty applied to a predictor you have strong prior reason to believe in — a dose, an established risk factor — shrinks it for the same reason it shrinks noise4, which is no reason at all. glmnet lets you exempt a predictor with penalty.factor, and almost nobody does; scikit-learn’s LogisticRegression offers no equivalent at all.

30.12 What you can and cannot now claim

Can: that you can fit a penalised logistic regression with the penalty chosen by cross-validation in either language, read off which coefficients it set to exactly zero, and state the AUC cost of the sparser model.

Cannot: that the variables the lasso kept are the variables that matter. The selection moved when the rows were resampled and the controls survived the penalty the cross-validation chose, so the variable list is an output of the procedure as much as of the biology. Nor can you claim the penalty is assumption-free: it encodes a preference for small coefficients that you did not derive from anything clinical. Chapter 32 asks what the model did, variable by variable and patient by patient, which is a different question and has its own controls.
