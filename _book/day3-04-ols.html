Exercise 3.4 — Ordinary least squares, in closed form

Objectives

  • Derive the ordinary least squares estimators for a single predictor, in your own handwriting, from the squared-error loss.
  • Compute both estimators from a dataset with nothing but means, a covariance and a variance, and show that they agree with lm().
  • Name the two conditions the derivation imposes on the fitted residuals, and check them numerically.

Why this, here

Exercise 3.3 — Gradient descent minimised the squared-error loss by walking downhill, and needed standardised columns and a well-chosen learning rate to get there. For this particular loss and this particular model, none of that is necessary: the minimum can be written down. This exercise does the algebra, and then checks it against the software.

NoteThis exercise is one language, deliberately

The derivation is algebra and has no R version or Python version. Only the verification at the end is code, and that appears in both languages. If you are following the booklet in Python, the three pages of mathematics below still apply to you in full — LinearRegression is solving the same equations as lm().

Setting up the minimisation problem

We want the intercept and slope that minimise the sum of squared residuals:

\[\min_{\hat{\beta_0}, \hat{\beta_1}} \; W, \qquad\text{where}\qquad W = \sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{\beta_0} - \hat{\beta_1} x_i)^2 \tag{12.1}\]

Minimising the sum and minimising the mean are the same problem: dividing by \(N\) scales the whole surface without moving its lowest point. We use the sum here because it keeps the algebra clean.

A minimum of a smooth function is where all its partial derivatives vanish. There are two parameters, so there are two equations.

\[\frac{\partial W}{\partial \hat{\beta_0}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} -2(y_i - \hat{\beta_0} - \hat{\beta_1} x_i) = 0 \tag{12.2}\]

\[\frac{\partial W}{\partial \hat{\beta_1}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} -2 x_i (y_i - \hat{\beta_0} - \hat{\beta_1} x_i) = 0 \tag{12.3}\]

Both follow from the chain rule: the inside of the bracket differentiates to \(-1\) with respect to \(\hat\beta_0\) and to \(-x_i\) with respect to \(\hat\beta_1\). These two are the normal equations, and everything below is rearrangement.

NoteWhere the gradients of Exercise 3.3 — Gradient descent came from

Equation 12.2 and Equation 12.3, divided by \(N\), are exactly the two gradients the descent loop used. The difference between the two exercises is not the mathematics; it is that here we set the derivatives to zero and solve, while there we evaluated them at the current guess and took a step.

Solving for \(\hat{\beta_0}\)

Divide Equation 12.2 by \(-2\) — which is allowed because the right-hand side is zero, and is the reason some textbooks write the loss with a \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) in front:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{\beta_0} - \hat{\beta_1} x_i) = 0\]

Distribute the summation over the three terms. The middle one is a constant summed \(N\) times:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i - N\hat{\beta_0} - \hat{\beta_1}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad N \hat{\beta_0} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i - \hat{\beta_1} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i\]

Divide by \(N\) and use \(\frac{1}{N}\sum y_i = \bar{y}\) and \(\frac{1}{N}\sum x_i = \bar{x}\):

\[\boxed{\;\hat{\beta_0} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta_1} \bar{x}\;}\]

Read that before going on: whatever the slope turns out to be, the fitted line passes through the point \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\). The mean of the predictors and the mean of the outcome are always on the line.

Solving for \(\hat{\beta_1}\)

Divide Equation 12.3 by \(-2\) as well, and expand:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( x_i y_i - \hat{\beta_0} x_i - \hat{\beta_1} x_i^2 \right) = 0\]

Substitute \(\hat{\beta_0} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta_1} \bar{x}\) from above:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i y_i - \sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bar{y} - \hat{\beta_1}\bar{x})x_i - \hat{\beta_1} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i^2 = 0\]

Distribute the sums, pulling the constants \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\) out in front:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i y_i - \bar{y}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i + \hat{\beta_1}\bar{x} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i - \hat{\beta_1} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i^2 = 0\]

Now collect the two terms in \(\hat{\beta_1}\) on one side and use \(\sum x_i = N\bar{x}\) and \(\sum y_i = N\bar{y}\):

\[\hat{\beta_1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i^2 - N\bar{x}^2\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i y_i - N\bar{x}\bar{y}\]

which, using the identity in the callout below for both sides, is

\[\hat{\beta_1} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})} {\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}\]

NoteThe identity, in six steps

\[\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i y_i - N \bar{x} \bar{y}\]

Step 1 — expand the product. \[\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( x_i y_i - x_i \bar{y} - \bar{x} y_i + \bar{x} \bar{y} \right)\]

Step 2 — separate the summation. \[= \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i y_i - \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i \bar{y} - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bar{x} y_i + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bar{x} \bar{y}\]

Step 3 — factor the constants out. \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\) do not depend on \(i\), so \[\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i \bar{y} = \bar{y} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bar{x} y_i = \bar{x} \sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bar{x} \bar{y} = N \bar{x} \bar{y}\]

Step 4 — substitute back. \[= \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i y_i - \bar{y} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i - \bar{x} \sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i + N \bar{x} \bar{y}\]

Step 5 — use \(\sum x_i = N \bar{x}\) and \(\sum y_i = N \bar{y}\). \[= \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i y_i - \bar{y} (N \bar{x}) - \bar{x} (N \bar{y}) + N \bar{x} \bar{y}\]

Step 6 — combine. The second and third terms are each \(-N\bar{x}\bar{y}\), and one of them cancels the fourth: \[= \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i y_i - N \bar{x} \bar{y}\]

Setting \(y = x\) gives the companion identity \(\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \sum x_i^2 - N\bar{x}^2\), which is the denominator.

So the complete solution, for one predictor, is two lines of arithmetic:

\[\hat{\beta_1} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)} \qquad\qquad \hat{\beta_0} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta_1} \bar{x}\]

No iteration, no learning rate, no starting guess. The derivation follows the one in Berkeley’s EEP118 handout; with more than one predictor the same argument is done in matrix form and gives \(\hat{\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y\), which is what lm() actually evaluates.

Both give 110.298 and 0.2886749. Not approximately: to every digit either language will print. lm() is not doing anything you have not just done by hand, and neither is LinearRegression.

NoteThe covariance ratio also explains Exercise 3.3 — Gradient descent

Standardising both columns makes \(\operatorname{Var}(x) = 1\) and turns \(\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)\) into the correlation, so on standardised data \(\hat\beta_1 = \operatorname{Cor}(x,y)\) — the 0.1347 the descent converged to. The correlation is the slope of the standardised fit, which is a more useful definition of it than the one most people carry.

The fitted residuals sum to something of the order of \(10^{-13}\) and their product with age to the order of \(10^{-11}\): zero, to the precision floating-point arithmetic allows. The hand-drawn line’s residuals sum to −1338.5 and its cross-product to −47700.5.

That is a diagnostic worth keeping. If someone hands you fitted values from a least-squares model and the residuals do not sum to zero, either there is no intercept in the model or those are not least-squares fitted values.

The result that should bother you

The derivation divides by \(\operatorname{Var}(x)\). It therefore returns an answer for any dataset in which the predictor is not perfectly constant — and says nothing about how large that variance is. Refit the same model on progressively narrower age bands.

  • R
  • Python
bands <- list(c(21, 25), c(21, 30), c(21, 40), c(21, 81))

do.call(rbind, lapply(bands, function(b) {
  s <- glu |> filter(age >= b[1], age <= b[2])
  data.frame(band = paste(b[1], "to", b[2]), n = nrow(s),
             var_age = var(s$age),
             slope = cov(s$age, s$glucose) / var(s$age))
}))
#>       band  n    var_age     slope
#> 1 21 to 25 32   1.910282 0.6812665
#> 2 21 to 30 48   8.074025 4.1727148
#> 3 21 to 40 59  21.303916 2.2139585
#> 4 21 to 81 80 190.398576 0.2886749
rows = []
for lo, hi in [(21, 25), (21, 30), (21, 40), (21, 81)]:
    s = glu[(glu["age"] >= lo) & (glu["age"] <= hi)]
    rows.append({"band": f"{lo} to {hi}", "n": len(s),
                 "var_age": s["age"].var(),
                 "slope": s["age"].cov(s["glucose"]) / s["age"].var()})
pd.DataFrame(rows)
band n var_age slope
0 21 to 25 32 1.910282 0.681266
1 21 to 30 48 8.074025 4.172715
2 21 to 40 59 21.303916 2.213958
3 21 to 81 80 190.398576 0.288675

Three steps of reasoning.

  1. Over the full range the slope is 0.289 mg/dL per year. Among patients aged 21 to 40 it is 2.21, and among those aged 21 to 30 it is 4.17 — fourteen times the full-range estimate, from a subset of the same 80 patients.
  2. The mechanism is the denominator. Restricting the age range cuts \(\operatorname{Var}(x)\) from 190 to 8, and the covariance in the numerator is estimated from fewer, closer points, so it is noise-dominated. The estimator is behaving exactly as derived; the data cannot support what is being asked of it.
  3. So a published slope is not a property of a relationship. It is a property of a relationship and the range of predictor values the sample happened to cover. When you compare your coefficient with someone else’s, compare the predictor ranges first.

Reflection

  1. The derivation never mentions the data being normally distributed, independent, or anything else. What exactly does it assume, and where in the algebra would you look to find out?
  2. Given that the closed form exists and takes microseconds, why is the whole of modern machine learning fitted by gradient descent?

What you can and cannot now claim

Can: that you can derive and compute the least squares estimators for one predictor, verify them against both languages’ implementations, and state the two conditions a least-squares fit imposes on its residuals.

Cannot: that any of today’s models are good. Every number on every page was computed on the rows the model was fitted to, and we have not scored a single held-out patient.
